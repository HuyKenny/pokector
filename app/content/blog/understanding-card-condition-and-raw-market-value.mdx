Two copies of the same card, same set, same holofoil — one sells for $80, the other
for $30. The difference is condition, and understanding it is essential to reading
any collection value honestly.

## The condition scale, in practice

- **Near Mint (NM)** — looks unplayed. Minimal edge wear visible only under close
  inspection. This is what published market prices describe.
- **Lightly Played (LP)** — minor edge/corner wear or light scratches visible on
  inspection, nothing distracting in a sleeve. Typically sells around 15–30% below NM.
- **Moderately Played (MP)** — obvious wear, moderate scratching or whitening,
  perhaps light creasing. Often 40–60% below NM.
- **Heavily Played (HP)** — major wear, creases, possible minor damage. Frequently
  70%+ below NM.
- **Damaged** — tears, water damage, writing, missing pieces. Worth a fraction, if
  buyers exist at all.

Percentages vary by card and market moment — expensive chase cards hold value in
lower grades better than bulk rares do.

## How to self-grade quickly

Check four things under bright light: **corners** (whitening?), **edges** (nicks,
silvering on dark borders?), **surface** (scratches on the holo under angled light?),
and **centering**. Tilt the card; holofoil scratches only reveal themselves at an
angle. When in doubt between two grades, record the lower one — future you prefers
conservative surprises.

## Why your tracker doesn't auto-discount

Pokector shows the **raw near-mint market estimate** for every card and lets you
record condition separately, without applying automatic multipliers. That is a
deliberate choice: real condition discounts are not a fixed formula — they differ by
card, price tier, and buyer. A tool that silently multiplies your MP Charizard by
0.5 is inventing a number and hiding the assumption. Better: show the honest market
reference, keep your condition data visible, and let you judge the discount where it
matters.

Practically, that means a collection full of played cards is worth _less_ than its
displayed estimate — treat the total as a ceiling, not an appraisal.

## When grading changes the math

Professional grading (PSA, BGS, CGC) moves a card into a different market entirely —
graded prices are not covered by raw-card estimates at all. Grading tends to make
sense when a card is genuinely near-mint-or-better _and_ the raw price is high
enough that the grading fee is small in comparison. Grading played bulk is a
donation to the grading company.

## The takeaway

Record condition for every card that matters, read displayed values as near-mint
references, and remember that the market prices _cards_, not spreadsheets. Honest
inputs are what make a collection tracker worth trusting.
